How to win at President

President — or Scum, or Asshole — looks like a game of luck: you get a hand, you empty it. It is really a game of management. Here is what separates the players who finish President from those who hand over their best cards next round.

These tips apply to the classic rules. If you don’t know them yet, start with the rules of President; the end of this page covers the Larbin variant, where a trick goes around the table only once.

Put these tips into practice

Against bots, right away — or in the daily challenge, the same deal for everyone.

Before you play: read your hand

1. Count your “exits”

Sort your hand into groups: pairs, three of a kind, and single cards. Each group is an exit, a play that brings you closer to the end. Thirteen cards that fit into six exits empty much faster than thirteen cards that are all odd ones.

Above all, remember this: your low cards almost never leave on their own. A lone 4 beats nobody; it only goes out if you are the one leading the trick. Every weak card is a trick you will have to win first.

2. Spot your masters

A master is a play nobody can beat: a 2, or an Ace once the 2s are gone, or a pair high enough for the moment. It is how you take the lead back — and dump one of your low cards. The maths is simple: you need roughly as many masters as weak exits to get rid of.

During the round

3. Lead low

When the lead comes back to you, use it to get rid of what wouldn’t go out otherwise: your lowest single or pair. Leading a Queen because you “have the lead” is a waste: that Queen could have beaten someone later.

4. Choose the format

Whoever leads sets the format of the trick. If you hold lots of pairs and the others seem short of them, lead a pair: those who have none must pass. Conversely, don’t lead three of a kind in a trick where you’ll need to take the lead back afterwards — you’ll have nothing left to do it with.

5. Don’t break a pair without a reason

Beating a lone Queen with one of a pair of Kings means spending two potential masters on a single trick. Keep the pair whole, unless it could never go out together anyway.

6. Passing is a weapon

Nothing forces you to go higher. Letting a trick go means keeping your strong cards for a moment when they really matter — typically, to take the lead back when you still have a low card to place. Beginners often lose by playing everything they can, as early as they can.

7. Count the 2s and the Aces

There are four 2s and four Aces in the deck. Know how many have been played: if three 2s are gone and you hold the fourth, it is a master until the end. If none have fallen, your Ace isn’t. Counting just the two highest ranks is already enough to change your decisions.

8. Watch who passes

A player who passes on a single 6 probably has no small singles left — or is keeping their big cards for later. The one who never follows with pairs is short of them. What the others refuse to play tells you as much as what they play.

9. Plan your way out

Three or four cards from the end, plan your last plays in order: which trick you will take back, with what, and what you will play next. Ideally, finish with a master, or with a lead you are sure to get. At many tables, finishing on a 2 is forbidden or punished: never keep a 2 as your last card.

Between rounds

10. Handle your exchanges

Where the President chooses which cards to hand back to the Scum, give back your isolated low cards — the ones that would each have cost you a trick — never a card that completes a pair. As the Scum, there is nothing to choose: your two best cards go. Expect it, and play the next round for the comeback, passing more often and waiting for your lead.

In Le Larbin: one lap changes everything

Le Larbin is a variant where everyone speaks only once per trick, the 2 cuts, and finishing on a 2 makes you the Lackey by default (see the rules). The tips above still hold, but three things change.

Your seat becomes decisive

Nobody comes back into a trick: whoever speaks last sees everyone else first, and wins the lead with the smallest card that does the job. Whoever speaks first can’t count on a second chance. A big card kept “to come back in later” won’t come out in this trick: play it at the right moment, or keep it for another.

The 2 is a shortcut, not a finish

Cutting with a 2 ends the trick on the spot and gives you the lead: it is the surest way to lead, and therefore to dump a low card. But a 2 as your last card condemns you. Play your 2s before the end, ideally to lead with what wouldn’t go out otherwise.

Memory pays

In Le Larbin, cards aren’t shuffled between rounds: the deck is gathered in the order the cards fell, and the Boss cuts wherever they like. A player who remembers the end of the previous round can guess where the strong cards lie — and the Boss can aim.

To practise: the daily challenge gives everyone the same deal each day, over three rounds. Replay your mistakes in your head, compare your score with the day’s leaderboard — it’s the best school there is.
