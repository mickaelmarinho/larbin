President variants

Ask four people for the rules of President and you’ll get five answers. The game spread by word of mouth, from summer camps to the end of family dinners, and every table kept its own habits. Here are the ones you meet most often.

For the basic rules — ranking, tricks, roles, exchanges — see the rules of President. This page only covers what changes from one table to the next.

One game, many names

In English it goes by President, Scum, Asshole, Capitalism or Kings and Peasants, among others — often named after the role nobody wants. In France it’s Président, or Trou du cul, and sometimes le Larbin — “the lackey”. They all descend from East Asian climbing games, of which the Japanese Daifugō — “the very rich” — is the closest cousin.

The most common house rules

Coming back after passing?

This is the biggest difference, and the one people forget to agree on before starting. At some tables, passing counts for the whole trick: you don’t play again until it’s over. At others, you can pass and then come back on the next lap, as long as someone keeps going higher. The first rule makes the game more tactical; the second, longer.

The skip

Playing a card of the same rank as the previous one — a 9 on a 9 — skips the next player’s turn. Depending on the table, it’s allowed instead of going higher, or only on top of it. It’s an annoying rule, which is why people love it.

Revolution

Playing four of a kind reverses the ranking: the 3 becomes the highest card and the 2 the lowest, until the end of the round or until the counter-revolution. It comes from Japanese Daifugō, where it’s called kakumei, and it’s the Scum’s best weapon: with a hand full of small cards, four of a kind is enough to turn the table upside down.

The 8 that ends it, the 2 that ends it

In Daifugō, playing an 8 ends the trick immediately, and whoever played it leads again. In the West it’s more often the 2 that plays this part: nothing can go higher anyway, so you might as well end it right away rather than wait for everyone to pass.

No finishing on a 2

Playing your last card as a 2 — or as a card that ends the trick, depending on the table — is forbidden, or punished: you become last whatever your finishing order. The rule stops players from saving their 2s until the end to finish with certainty.

Runs

Some tables allow runs: three or more consecutive cards (5-6-7), sometimes required to be of the same suit. You then have to answer with a higher run of the same length. Runs make odd-card hands much easier to empty.

Jokers

When played with jokers, they become either the highest cards, above the 2s, or wild cards that complete a pair or three of a kind. Either way, they mostly benefit whoever receives them… which the Scum has to give away.

Who leads the first round

Depending on the table: whoever holds the queen of hearts, whoever holds the lowest 3, or the dealer. After that, the previous round’s President usually leads — except at tables that give this advantage to the Scum, to help them climb back.

The exchanges

Almost everyone exchanges two cards between President and Scum, one between Vice-President and Vice-Scum. What varies is the choice: does the President hand back cards of their choice, or necessarily their lowest? The first version rewards the President a second time; the second is more mechanical, and fairer.

Scoring, or not

Many play endlessly, simply swapping roles each round. Others keep score — as many points as players left behind you — and stop at a total agreed in advance.

Le Larbin’s choices

Le Larbin is a house variant that settled each of these questions — often in the fastest-paced way.
